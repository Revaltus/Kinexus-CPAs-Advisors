A developer in Washington County takes the keys to a new 24-unit apartment building. Within a month, their CPA asks the general contractor for something nobody planned for: a cost breakdown detailed enough to separate the cabinetry, the specialty electrical, the parking lot, and the site utilities from the building shell. The contractor has a final pay application, a stack of lien waivers, and a schedule of values with 40 lines on it. The developer's cost segregation provider needs 400. Weeks of back-and-forth follow, the study fee climbs, and the developer remembers who made it harder.

That is the opening for a cost-segregation-ready closeout package. A contractor who hands over organized, itemized project records at turnover gives the owner a tax-saving asset, not just a finished building. Few contractors in St George do this, which makes it an easy way to stand out. This article covers what the package contains, how to build it into your workflow from preconstruction, how to position it, and why it raises the value of your company.

## Why developers care about your closeout documents

A new commercial or residential rental building is normally depreciated over 27.5 years (residential rental) or 39 years (nonresidential). A cost segregation study breaks that building into its components and reclassifies the ones that qualify as shorter-lived property: 5-year, 7-year, and 15-year assets. Think specialty electrical and plumbing serving equipment, certain finishes, millwork, and land improvements such as paving, landscaping, site lighting, and drainage. Shorter lives mean faster deductions.

The percentage reclassified varies by property type and by how the building was designed. Reclassification in the range of 20 to 35 percent of the depreciable cost is common for many property types, and amenity-heavy or finish-heavy projects can run higher. Here is a simple illustration:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Depreciable building cost | $6,000,000 |
| Reclassified to shorter-lived property (25%) | $1,500,000 |
| First-year deduction on that portion at 100% bonus depreciation | $1,500,000 |
| Federal tax effect for an owner at a 37% rate | about $555,000 deferred |

The word is deferred, and it matters. Faster depreciation shifts deductions earlier. It does not erase tax, and depreciation recapture can apply when the property is sold. Passive activity rules and real estate professional status also decide who can use the deductions right away. A good developer already knows this, and their CPA will model it. What the developer wants from you is the raw material for the study.

### Timing keeps the topic live

Bonus depreciation has changed several times in recent years. It phased down under the 2017 tax law to 80 percent for 2023 and 60 percent for 2024. The 2025 legislation commonly called OBBBA restored 100 percent bonus depreciation for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025. The details depend on acquisition and placed-in-service dates, and construction projects that straddle those dates need a careful read. Developers are asking their advisors about it, and the question quickly lands on the contractor's records. Our piece on [why the placed-in-service date decides the deduction](/resources/the-construction-owners-deadline-why-equipment-purchases-mad) covers the same principle for equipment. It applies to buildings too.

## What the study provider needs from you

Most cost segregation studies are prepared by engineering-based specialists, not by the contractor and not by the CPA. The IRS has published an audit techniques guide on cost segregation that favors studies built from actual cost records over rough percentage estimates. In plain terms, the closer a study sits to your real, itemized costs, the easier it is to defend. When the records are thin, the provider has to estimate, which takes more hours and leaves the owner with more audit risk.

Here is what a provider typically looks for, and why each item matters:

| Document | What it does for the study |
|---|---|
| Detailed schedule of values (itemized, not lump-sum lines) | Lets the provider assign cost to individual components |
| Final cost report by cost code | Ties the study total back to what the owner actually paid |
| Change order log with scope descriptions | Shows where costs were added, removed, or moved between categories |
| Subcontractor contracts and invoices | Supports allocation of trade costs such as electrical, plumbing, and HVAC |
| Material and equipment invoices | Separates owner-specified fixtures and equipment from building structure |
| As-built drawings and finish schedules | Lets the engineer identify what was actually installed |
| Site work breakdown | Isolates paving, utilities, landscaping, lighting, and drainage |
| Closeout dates and certificate of occupancy | Supports the placed-in-service date |

A lump-sum line reading "Electrical: $412,000" is the most common problem. The provider cannot tell how much went to general building lighting, how much to dedicated circuits for kitchen equipment or a server room, and how much to site lighting. Without an itemized breakdown, they guess, or they come back to you with questions months after your crew has moved on.

## Build the package from preconstruction onward

The worst time to assemble this package is the week after turnover. By then the project manager is on the next job, the superintendent has left, and half the backup is in someone's truck. The workable approach is to set it up at the start so the package assembles itself as the job progresses.

### At bid and contract stage

Ask your estimator to structure the schedule of values with the study in mind. Separate site work from building work. Break out interior finishes, millwork, and specialty systems rather than rolling them into a single division line. This does not change your price. It changes how you present it.

Write the closeout deliverable into the contract or the project manual, with the format and timing specified. If you are bidding on a project where the owner already has a study provider lined up, ask what format they prefer and build to it.

### During construction

Code your job cost system so the cost codes map to the schedule of values. Your accountant can help with this setup, and it is one of the places where construction bookkeeping and tax strategy meet. If your cost codes and your billing lines do not match, someone has to reconcile them by hand at the end.

Track change orders with a short written scope description each time, not just a dollar amount. "CO 7: add 14 exterior wall packs and conduit run to east parking area" tells a provider exactly where $18,400 belongs. "CO 7: electrical, $18,400" tells them nothing.

File subcontractor and supplier invoices by cost code as they arrive. Request itemized backup from subs on pay applications when their scope is large or mixed. A plumbing sub who installs both domestic water and a grease interceptor for a restaurant tenant improvement should show those as separate lines.

### At substantial completion

Collect as-built drawings and finish schedules while the design team is still engaged. Record the date of the certificate of occupancy and the date the owner took beneficial use. Compile everything into one folder structure, delivered digitally, with a one-page index. Secure digital document handling matters here as well, because these files include vendor pricing and payment records you do not want circulating by unsecured email.

## A sample closeout checklist

Adapt this to your project types. A ground-up multifamily building and a medical office tenant improvement will not need identical items.

- Itemized schedule of values with site work separated from building work
- Final job cost report by cost code, reconciled to the final pay application
- Change order log with written scope descriptions and cost impact
- Executed subcontracts and itemized final invoices for each major trade
- Material and equipment invoices for owner-selected fixtures, appliances, and specialty items
- Allowances reconciliation showing what was spent against each allowance
- As-built drawings, finish schedules, and equipment lists
- Site work breakdown: paving, curbs, utilities, landscaping, irrigation, lighting, drainage
- Certificate of occupancy and substantial completion date
- Final lien waivers and retainage release documentation
- Warranty and O&M manuals (useful for identifying installed systems)
- One-page index listing every file and its location

Retainage deserves a specific note. Owners and providers need to know the full contract cost, including amounts held and released after turnover. If you are managing it carefully, our guide to [planning around retainage](/retainage-is-holding-your-cash-hostage-heres-how-to-plan-around-it) will help you keep the final numbers clean.

## Documentation mistakes that cost owners money

These are the problems that most often turn a two-week study into a two-month one.

1. **Lump-sum trade lines.** Described above. Fix them at the schedule of values stage, not at closeout.
2. **Change orders with no scope description.** A dollar amount without a narrative cannot be classified.
3. **Costs that do not tie out.** If the study total differs from the owner's books and your final cost report, the provider has to reconcile the gap, and the owner pays for the time.
4. **Missing owner-direct purchases.** Owners often buy appliances, signage, or equipment directly. Your records may not show them, and the study is incomplete unless someone flags them.
5. **Site work buried in building costs.** Paving, utilities, and landscaping often qualify for 15-year treatment. When they are rolled into general conditions or building lines, the owner may lose that benefit.
6. **Delivery months after turnover.** Records requested a year later rarely come back complete. The superintendent has moved on and the subs have stopped answering.
7. **No placed-in-service date record.** Depreciation starts when the property is ready and available for its intended use. A certificate of occupancy and a documented turnover date prevent disputes.

## Price it or position it

There are three reasonable ways to handle this deliverable, and the right one depends on your market and project size.

**Include it as standard.** For most St George contractors working with repeat developers, this is the best choice. The marginal cost is small once your cost codes and schedule of values are built correctly, and you gain a visible point of difference. You are not adding labor, you are organizing work you already do.

**Offer it as a line item on larger or complex projects.** If a project needs extra work, such as reconstructing costs from a messy owner-direct purchasing process or compiling site work separately, a modest fee for closeout documentation is fair. Be specific about what it covers.

**Tier it.** Some contractors offer a standard closeout set and an enhanced cost-segregation-ready set. This works when you build both spec homes and commercial work, because needs differ.

Whichever you choose, state it plainly in the proposal. A line saying "Closeout package formatted for cost segregation study, delivered within 30 days of substantial completion" gets noticed by a developer reading five proposals side by side.

## How to talk about it with developers

You do not need to be a tax expert in the conversation. You need to show you understand what the owner's advisor will ask for. Three practical places to raise it:

- **In the proposal or interview.** One short paragraph: your team builds the schedule of values and cost coding so the owner's cost segregation provider can work from actual project costs. Developers who have been through a painful study will remember that.
- **At the preconstruction meeting.** Ask who the owner's tax advisor and study provider are, and whether they have format preferences. Few contractors ask. It shows you are thinking about the owner's after-construction position.
- **At turnover.** Hand over the package in person, walk through the index, and tell the owner's CPA how to reach your project accountant with questions.

Medical office developers, restaurant and retail operators building out spaces, and investors building rental communities all benefit. The conversation sounds different for each, but the principle is the same: you are protecting the value of what they paid for.

When a buyer or lender looks at a contracting business, they look for things that do not depend on the owner personally. A documented, repeatable closeout process is one of them. It shows disciplined job costing, organized project accounting, and a client experience that keeps developers coming back.

Repeat developer relationships produce more predictable backlog. Referrals from a developer's CPA or study provider add a channel that does not require marketing spend. ## Common Questions

**Does the contractor prepare the cost segregation study?**

No. The study is usually prepared by an engineering-based specialist hired by the property owner, and the owner's CPA applies the results to the tax return. Your role is to supply organized, itemized cost records so the provider can work from actual costs instead of estimates, which lowers the fee and the audit risk.

**Is bonus depreciation still available for new construction?**

Yes. Legislation enacted in 2025 (OBBBA) restored 100 percent bonus depreciation for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025. Eligibility depends on acquisition, construction start, and placed-in-service dates, and passive loss limits can affect who uses the deduction. The owner's CPA should confirm the details for each project.

**What is the most common closeout mistake contractors make?**

Lump-sum trade lines on the schedule of values, such as a single "Electrical" or "Sitework" amount. A study provider cannot allocate cost to individual components from a single figure. Itemizing site work, finishes, and specialty systems at bid stage costs little and saves the owner weeks of rework later.
