Most contractors in St George know their big tax dates. It's the quiet ones that cost money: a workers' comp policy that renewed at a higher rate because nobody asked for quotes, an equipment lease that rolled into another 36 months, a license that lapsed the week a bid was due. None of these is a hard problem. They are calendar problems, and December is the best month to solve them, because you are already looking back at the year and forward to the next one.

This post walks through how to build a business compliance calendar for a construction company in St George, what belongs on it, and which dates to pin down before January.

## Why December is the right month to build the calendar

By December, you know what the year actually looked like. You know which jobs ran over, which subs you'd hire again, and whether payroll grew from 8 people to 14. That is exactly the information you need to decide what your insurance, contracts, and licenses should look like next year.

December also has a hard edge that other months don't. Equipment must be placed in service by December 31 to count for the current tax year, and Q4 estimated payments and 1099 and W-2 filings are due within a few weeks of the new year. A calendar built now lets those deadlines sit next to your operational dates instead of ambushing you separately.

A year-end business planning checklist usually stops at taxes. For a contractor, that misses half the picture. Your ability to bid, bond, and get paid depends on policies, licenses, and contract terms that have nothing to do with a tax return.

## Start with insurance and bonding, because they set your ability to bid

Insurance is the first thing to date-stamp, since renewals often arrive with little warning and a higher number. A business insurance renewal review works best when it starts 60 to 90 days before the policy expires, not the week before.

For a St George contractor, the list typically looks like this:

- **General liability.** Confirm your reported revenue and subcontractor costs are accurate. Premiums are often rated on these figures, and overstating them is paying for nothing.
- **Workers' compensation.** Premiums are based on payroll by class code and are audited after the term. If your framers, finish carpenters, and office staff are not in the right classes, you can overpay or owe a surprise audit bill.
- **Commercial auto and equipment.** Trucks and trailers get bought and sold all year. Make sure the schedule matches what is actually in your yard.
- **Builder's risk and umbrella.** Check limits against the size of the jobs you plan to pursue next year, not the ones you finished last year.
- **Surety bonds.** If you bid public work or larger commercial projects, your surety will want year-end financials and a current WIP schedule before raising your bonding capacity.

That last item is where accounting and compliance meet. A banker or bonding agent reads your work-in-progress schedule closely, and we explain why in [what a WIP schedule tells you and why your banker cares](/what-a-wip-schedule-actually-tells-you-and-why-your-banker-cares). If the schedule is clean and ready in December, your bonding conversation in January is a short one.

Here is a simple illustration. Say a 12-person framing contractor takes in $3.2 million in revenue, but the insurance application still lists last year's lower figure and an old payroll split. Everything looks fine until the audit true-up, which arrives months later. Ten minutes comparing the application to the year-end financials in December would have caught it.

## Pull your contracts and leases before they renew themselves

Auto-renewals are where small, forgettable agreements turn into multi-year commitments. Build a short inventory in December and note the notice window for each one.

![Business owner reading through a contract at a desk before signing](/content-assets/reviewing-contract-documents-desk.jpg)

Look at:

- Equipment leases and rental agreements, including the end-of-term options and any early buyout language.
- Yard, shop, or office leases, especially escalation clauses and renewal notice deadlines.
- Supplier agreements and trade credit terms. Net 30 can become net 15 if you miss a review.
- Software subscriptions for estimating, project management, and time tracking. Most of these renew annually, and you may be paying for seats you no longer use.
- Master subcontract agreements and your standard customer contract.

The customer contract deserves its own look. Retainage terms, payment timelines, change order language, and release conditions all affect cash. A 10 percent retainage clause on a $1.5 million job holds $150,000 of your money until closeout, and the contract determines when it is released. If you have been accepting whatever the developer's paper says, December is a good time to decide which terms you will push back on in the next year. Our piece on [planning around retainage](/retainage-is-holding-your-cash-hostage-heres-how-to-plan-around-it) shows how to forecast that cash so it does not catch you short.

Have your attorney review contract language. Your CPA's job here is to flag the financial terms and model what they do to your cash flow.

## Licenses, permits, and state filings that expire quietly

A business license renewal checklist for a contractor has more moving parts than most owners expect. The SBA keeps a useful overview of the [licenses and permits businesses typically need](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/apply-licenses-permits), and it is a good starting point for confirming nothing is missing.

For a construction company in Washington County, put these on the calendar with the actual expiration date and the person responsible:

1. **Contractor license.** Check your expiration and continuing education requirements on the Utah Division of Professional Licensing website. Do not rely on memory, because renewal cycles are not annual.
2. **Qualifier and trade licenses.** If one person holds the qualifying license for the company, note what happens if that person leaves.
3. **City and county business licenses.** St George, Washington County, and neighboring cities each have their own renewal timing if you work across jurisdictions.
4. **Annual state report deadlines.** Utah business entities renew with the state each year, generally by the end of the anniversary month of registration. Confirm your month on the state's business portal and put it in your calendar with a reminder 30 days earlier.
5. **Registered agent and entity details.** If you moved the shop or changed officers, update them before the renewal, not after.
6. **Sales and use tax, withholding, and unemployment accounts.** Make sure each has a filing frequency noted, since some businesses move between monthly and quarterly filing as they grow.

Missing a state renewal can put an entity out of good standing, which causes problems when a lender, surety, or general contractor asks for proof. It takes a few minutes to prevent and a lot longer to fix.

## A sample compliance calendar for a St George contractor

Here is a starting template. Your dates will differ, so treat this as a scaffold and replace the placeholders with your own.

| When | What to do | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| December | Review insurance, place equipment in service, assemble year-end WIP and financials | Deduction timing and bonding capacity |
| January 15 | Q4 estimated tax payment due for owners | Avoids underpayment penalties |
| January 31 | W-2s, 1099-NECs, and Q4 payroll filings due | Late filings carry per-form penalties |
| February to March | Send financials to surety and bank, close out job cost reviews | Sets next year's credit and bonding limits |
| March 15 | S corporation and partnership returns due (or extend) | Pass-through owners need these before their personal returns |
| April 15 | Individual returns and Q1 estimates due | Q1 payment is easy to forget |
| Quarterly | Payroll tax filings, sales tax if applicable, estimate review | Keeps cash and tax in sync |
| Anniversary month | Utah annual entity renewal | Keeps the entity in good standing |
| 90 days before each expiration | Insurance and license renewal reviews | Time to compare options and fix errors |
| 60 days before notice window | Lease and contract auto-renewal check | Lets you renegotiate instead of accept |

When a date falls on a weekend or holiday, the due date generally moves to the next business day, but build your internal deadline a week earlier anyway. The [IRS Small Business and Self-Employed center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) is a good place to confirm current federal dates for your entity type.

## Tie the tax dates to the same calendar

Operational and tax dates belong in one place because they affect each other. A truck purchase affects insurance, financing, and depreciation. A new hire changes payroll filings, workers' comp classification, and your estimated tax projection.

The clearest example is year-end equipment. The placed-in-service date, not the invoice date, decides which year you take the deduction. If you plan to buy an excavator or a work truck, December is the deadline that matters, and we cover the mechanics in [why equipment bought after December 31 costs you real money](/the-construction-owners-deadline-why-equipment-purchases-mad). Put the purchase decision on the calendar for early December so delivery delays do not push it into the next year.

## Common Questions

**What is a business compliance calendar?**

It is a single schedule of every recurring date your company must meet: tax filings, state renewals, licenses, insurance and contract renewals, and payroll deadlines. For contractors it should also include bonding reviews and lease notice windows. The point is to see conflicts and gaps in one place, with a named owner for each item.

**When should a contractor start a business insurance renewal review?**

Start 60 to 90 days before each policy expires. That is enough time to correct revenue and payroll figures, get competing quotes, and ask your agent about changes in coverage. Workers' comp is worth checking against your payroll by class code, since premiums are audited after the term ends.

**What are the annual state report deadlines for a Utah business?**

Utah entities renew annually with the state, generally by the end of the month in which they were originally registered. Check your entity's anniversary month on the state business portal, then set a reminder 30 days ahead. Each entity you own has its own date, so a multi-entity owner may have several.

**Who should own the compliance calendar?**

One person inside the company should own it, typically the office manager or controller, with your CPA reviewing the tax and financial dates. Shared ownership usually means no ownership. The owner of the calendar should have the authority to chase documents and make sure renewals are actually completed.
